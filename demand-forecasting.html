Completed project / Retail forecasting

Retail Demand ForecastingTurning weekly demand into planning decisions.

I cleaned retail transaction data, tested five forecasting methods on five selected products and built a live Streamlit dashboard. It connects each product’s recommended model, forecast error and demand patterns to practical inventory planning actions.

  • Python
  • pandas
  • statsmodels
  • Streamlit

Independent portfolio project · Online Retail II data

Explore the live app ↗

The business question

Which forecasts can planners use?

The Online Retail II workbook contains more than one million raw transaction rows, including cancellations, adjustments, fees and duplicate records. These are not all genuine merchandise demand. I prepared a weekly demand series, screened products for sufficient history and regularity, and compared methods on a 12-week time-based hold-out.

The goal was a product-level recommendation with its uncertainty visible, rather than one default forecast for every SKU.

My approach

Clean, compare, then explain.

Prepare demand

I removed duplicate transactions and the overlapping December 2010 records, excluded non-merchandise and invalid-price lines, and matched same-day cancellations to reversed sales. The cleaned data contains 1,004,262 merchandise-order rows.

Test forecasts

I aggregated demand weekly and screened for at least 52 weeks of history, demand in at least 70% of weeks and moderate volatility. For five selected products, I compared naive, moving-average, seasonal naive, simple exponential smoothing and Holt trend forecasts.

Support decisions

I scored the 12-week hold-out with MAE, RMSE and WMAPE, using WMAPE to compare products with different demand volumes. The app shows model selection, forecast error, demand history and product-specific planning actions.

What I found

The best method varies by product.

Exponential smoothing had the lowest WMAPE for products 84879 (26.62%) and 84077 (38.40%). Holt trend performed best for 21212 (41.55%) and 85099B (41.75%). The naive baseline won for 85123A (42.05%), showing that a more complex method does not always improve the forecast.

Products 84879 and 84077 also had the highest peak-to-average demand ratios. Their lower average forecast errors do not remove the inventory risk from occasional spikes. I recommend more frequent review for higher-error products and additional stock protection for spike-prone products, with those actions tested against service and inventory outcomes.

Decision brief

Match the planning response to the product.

Review higher-error SKUs

The best test WMAPE ranged from 26.62% to 42.05%. Demand Planning should review higher-error products more often rather than treating every point forecast as equally dependable.

Track rolling WMAPE, forecast bias and stock outcomes by SKU.

Test protection for spikes

84879 and 84077 had the two lowest WMAPE values but the highest peak-to-average demand ratios. Inventory managers should test buffers against lead times and service targets.

Track peak-week availability, stockouts and excess stock.

Refresh model selection

The winning method differed across five SKUs, and the naive baseline won for 85123A. Analytics should repeat time-based comparisons as new observations and products enter scope.

Track model WMAPE and bias on rolling test periods.

These are planning tests, not measured improvements to inventory outcomes. The back-test covers five screened products and a 12-week hold-out.

Limitations & evidence

Use the forecast with context.

The model uses historical demand without promotions, prices or holidays as explanatory inputs. Five screened products were forecast, so the results do not describe the full catalogue. The recommended planning actions have not been tested for their effect on stockouts or excess inventory.

The cleaning logic, notebooks, model results and app code are available in the Retail Demand Forecasting repository.
